# Crop Diseases: Symptoms and Management

Sources: TNAU Agritech Portal crop protection pages (paddy, maize, chilli, tomato, onion, groundnut); Vikaspedia IPM for cereals (ragi blast); ICAR-IIMR state recommendations for maize disease management; NIPHM IPM package for chilli; UC IPM and Kerala Agricultural University notes on onion purple blotch; Coffee Board of India (coffee leaf rust). Review status: starter content, to be checked by the local agriculture office or Krishi Vigyan Kendra.

This document explains how to recognise common crop diseases and what a farmer can do without chemicals. It does not give fungicide or pesticide names or doses. For a spray, the farmer should show a sample of the diseased plant to the agriculture officer, Raitha Samparka Kendra or Krishi Vigyan Kendra, who will recommend the right product and dose for that crop.

## How to tell fungus, bacteria and virus diseases apart
Fungal diseases usually show spots with rings or a coloured border, powdery or downy growth, or rotting that spreads in wet, humid weather. Bacterial diseases often show water-soaked spots, yellow streaks from the leaf tip, a slimy ooze, or sudden wilting of a green plant. Virus diseases show curling, crinkling, mosaic (patchy light and dark green) or yellowing of leaves and stunted plants; viruses are spread by insects such as whitefly, thrips and aphids, and sprays cannot cure a plant that already has a virus. Nutrient shortage can look like disease: yellowing that is even across the older leaves of the whole field is more often a nutrient problem, while spots and patches that spread from plant to plant are more often a disease.

## General steps to prevent crop diseases
Use healthy, certified seed and disease-resistant varieties recommended for the district. Treat seed before sowing as advised by the agriculture office. Rotate crops so the same crop or crop family is not grown in the same field season after season. Keep proper spacing so air moves between plants, and do not over-irrigate or let water stand. Avoid too much nitrogen fertilizer, because soft, lush growth catches many diseases more easily. Remove and destroy diseased plants and crop residues, and keep the field and bunds free of weeds that carry disease. Control the insects that spread viruses. Clean tools after working in a diseased field. Watch the crop every few days, especially in cloudy, humid or rainy weather, and act early.

## Paddy: blast
Blast is caused by a fungus. Spindle-shaped (eye-shaped) spots with grey or whitish centres and brown borders appear on the leaves; many spots join and the leaves dry up. At the neck of the panicle the stem turns black and breaks (neck blast), so the grains stay empty. Blast spreads in cool nights, high humidity, long dew periods and fields given too much nitrogen. Management: grow resistant varieties, avoid excess nitrogen and apply it in splits, remove grass weeds on bunds, and destroy infected straw and stubble. If spots are spreading, ask the agriculture officer about a spray.

## Paddy: sheath blight
Sheath blight is a fungal disease. Oval, greenish-grey spots with a brown edge appear on the leaf sheath near the water line, then spread upwards; small brown hard bodies (sclerotia) may be seen. It is worse in thick planting, high nitrogen and warm, humid weather. Management: keep proper spacing, avoid excess nitrogen, remove weeds, plough in or destroy stubble after harvest, and drain the field for a few days if the disease is spreading.

## Paddy: bacterial leaf blight
Bacterial leaf blight starts at the leaf tips and edges as water-soaked, yellowish streaks that turn straw-coloured and dry; drops of milky or yellow ooze can be seen in the morning. Young plants may wilt completely (kresek). It spreads with rain, wind, flood water and from field to field through irrigation water. Management: grow resistant varieties, avoid excess nitrogen, do not let water flow from a diseased field into a healthy one, remove weed hosts, and keep the field drained for a few days when the disease appears.

## Paddy: brown spot
Brown spot is a fungal disease. Small oval brown spots with a grey centre and a yellow halo appear on leaves and grains, and grains may be discoloured. It is common in poor, nutrient-deficient soils and under drought stress. Management: use healthy treated seed, apply balanced fertilizer based on a soil test, and keep proper water in the field.

## Ragi (finger millet): blast
Ragi blast is caused by a fungus and can attack the crop at any stage. On the leaves it makes spots with grey centres and brown borders that join and dry the leaf. In neck blast the neck of the ear head turns black and the ear head dries or breaks. In finger blast the fingers turn brown from the tip to the base and the grains do not fill. The fungus is carried in seed, and the disease is worse in dense planting, high humidity and high nitrogen. Management: use resistant varieties and treated seed, avoid dense planting, do not apply excess nitrogen, and remove infected plants and crop residues. Ask the agriculture officer if the disease spreads.

## Maize: turcicum leaf blight
Turcicum leaf blight is a fungal disease. Long, cigar-shaped, greyish-green to tan spots appear on the leaves, starting on the lower leaves; many spots together make the leaves look burnt. It spreads in cool, humid and wet weather. Management: grow resistant hybrids, rotate maize with other crops, and bury or destroy infected stubble after harvest. If the spots appear early and spread, ask the agriculture officer about a spray.

## Maize: downy mildew
Downy mildew is caused by a fungus-like organism. Leaves show pale yellow or white stripes, and a white downy growth is seen on the underside of the leaves in the early morning. Infected plants are stunted, and the tassel may turn leafy (crazy top), so the plant gives little or no cob. Management: grow resistant hybrids, use treated seed, pull out and destroy infected plants early (roguing), and avoid sowing maize next to an infected sorghum or maize field.

## Tomato: bacterial wilt
In bacterial wilt the plant suddenly wilts while the leaves are still green, first in the hot afternoon and then permanently. If a cut piece of the lower stem is put in a glass of clear water, milky white threads stream out of it. The bacteria live in the soil and spread with irrigation water and on tools. Management: remove and destroy wilted plants with the soil around the roots, rotate tomato with non-related crops such as cereals for several seasons, avoid fields where wilt occurred before, use resistant varieties or grafted seedlings where available, and avoid waterlogging.

## Tomato and vegetable nurseries: damping off
Damping off kills seedlings in the nursery. Before they come up the seeds rot, or after they come up the young stems become soft and water-soaked at the soil level and the seedlings fall over and die in patches. It is caused by soil fungi and is worse in wet, poorly drained, crowded nurseries. Management: raise seedlings on raised beds with good drainage, use treated seed, do not overwater, sow thinly, and use clean soil or growing medium.

## Onion: purple blotch
Purple blotch is a fungal disease. Small, sunken, whitish spots appear on the leaves and become larger, with purple centres and rings, surrounded by a yellow halo. Spots join and girdle the leaf, and the leaf tip dries and falls over. It is worse in warm, humid and rainy weather, and thrips damage makes infection easier. Management: use disease-free seed and sets, rotate onion with other crops for a few seasons, remove crop debris and volunteer onions, keep good spacing and drainage, and control thrips.

## Potato: early blight
Early blight is a fungal disease. Dark brown spots with rings like a target (concentric rings) appear first on the older, lower leaves; the leaves turn yellow and dry. Tubers may show dark, sunken patches. It is worse when plants are stressed by poor nutrition or dry spells followed by humid weather. Management: use healthy seed tubers, give balanced fertilizer, rotate potato with non-related crops, remove infected leaves and crop residues, and avoid tomato and potato in the same field one after another. Late blight of potato is described in the pest management document.

## Chilli: leaf curl
Chilli leaf curl is a virus disease. Leaves curl, crinkle and become small and thick, the plant becomes bushy and stunted, and few flowers and fruits form. The virus is spread by whitefly, and thrips and mites cause similar leaf curling. Management: raise seedlings under insect-proof net, pull out and destroy curled plants early, remove weeds that host whitefly, use yellow sticky traps, and grow border crops such as maize or sorghum around the field. A plant that already has the virus cannot be cured.

## Chilli: die back and fruit rot (anthracnose)
Die back and fruit rot is caused by a fungus that is carried in seed. Tender twigs dry from the tip backwards (die back). On ripening fruits, sunken, round, dark spots with rings of black dots appear, and the fruits rot and fall. It spreads in warm, humid and rainy weather. Management: use healthy treated seed from disease-free fruits, remove and destroy dried twigs and rotten fruits, keep good spacing and drainage, and rotate chilli with non-related crops.

## Chilli and vegetables: powdery mildew
Powdery mildew is a fungal disease. White, powdery patches appear on the leaves, usually on the underside first in chilli; the leaves turn yellow and fall. It is common in dry days with cool, humid nights. Management: keep proper spacing, remove badly infected leaves, avoid excess nitrogen, and ask the agriculture officer about a spray if it spreads.

## Groundnut: tikka leaf spot
Tikka (early and late leaf spot) is a fungal disease of groundnut. Round brown or black spots, often with a yellow halo, appear on the leaves; many spots cause the leaves to fall early, which lowers pod yield. It is worse in humid, rainy weather. Management: use resistant varieties, rotate groundnut with cereals, remove and destroy crop residues, and keep proper spacing.

## Banana: Sigatoka leaf spot and Panama wilt
In Sigatoka leaf spot, small yellow streaks on the leaves grow into long brown spots with grey centres, and large parts of the leaf dry. Management: remove and destroy badly spotted leaves, keep good drainage and spacing, and remove weeds. In Panama wilt (Fusarium wilt), the older leaves turn yellow from the edges and hang down around the stem, and a cut through the base of the stem shows brown or reddish streaks. The fungus lives in the soil for many years. Management: plant disease-free suckers or tissue culture plants of tolerant varieties, remove and destroy wilted plants, avoid waterlogging, and do not move soil or suckers from a wilted field.

## Coffee: leaf rust
Coffee leaf rust is a fungal disease, mostly of arabica coffee. Small pale yellow spots appear on the upper side of the leaves, and orange-yellow powder forms on the underside of the same spots; badly affected leaves fall early, which weakens the plant and lowers the next crop. It spreads in warm, wet monsoon weather. Management: grow rust-tolerant varieties recommended by the Coffee Board, keep shade and plant nutrition balanced, and follow the Coffee Board extension advice for protective sprays.

## When to ask the agriculture officer
Ask the agriculture officer, Raitha Samparka Kendra or Krishi Vigyan Kendra when a disease is spreading quickly, when many plants wilt or die, when you are not sure which disease it is, or before buying any fungicide or pesticide. Take a few diseased plants or leaves, including healthy and affected parts, in a paper bag or a clear photo, and tell them the crop, its age, the variety and the recent weather. Always read the label and use protective clothing if a spray is recommended.
