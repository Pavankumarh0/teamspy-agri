# Types of Soil

Sources: ICAR - National Bureau of Soil Survey and Land Use Planning (NBSS&LUP) soil resource information for India and Karnataka; NCERT Geography (Soils of India); TNAU Agritech Portal soil science pages; Soil Health Card scheme guidelines. Review status: starter content, to be checked by the local agriculture office.

## Main types of soil in India
India has six main soil groups: alluvial soil, black soil, red soil, laterite soil, arid (desert) soil and forest or mountain soil. Saline and alkaline soils are found in patches in many regions. In Karnataka the most common soils are red soils, black soils and laterite soils, with coastal alluvial and sandy soils along the coast and forest soils in the Western Ghats.

## Red soil
Red soil gets its red colour from iron. It forms from old granite and gneiss rocks and is the most common soil in south and east Karnataka, including Bengaluru, Kolar, Tumakuru, Chikkaballapura, Mandya, Mysuru and Chitradurga districts. Red sandy and red loamy soils are light, porous and well drained, so they dry quickly and hold little water. They are usually low in nitrogen, phosphorus, organic matter and often zinc, and are slightly acidic to neutral. Crops: ragi, groundnut, pulses, millets, tomato, potato, vegetables, mulberry, mango and other fruit trees. Care: add plenty of farmyard manure or compost to hold water and nutrients, use mulching, irrigate lightly and often, and follow the soil test for fertilizer.

## Black soil (regur or cotton soil)
Black soil is dark, clay-rich and very fertile. It forms from basalt (Deccan trap) rock and is found in north Karnataka, for example Dharwad, Belagavi, Vijayapura, Kalaburagi, Bagalkot, Raichur and Haveri districts. It holds a lot of water, swells and becomes sticky when wet, and shrinks with deep cracks when dry. It is usually rich in calcium, magnesium and potassium but low in nitrogen, phosphorus and organic matter, and its pH is neutral to alkaline. Crops: cotton, jowar, wheat, bengal gram, tur, sunflower, safflower, sugarcane, chilli and onion. Care: do not over-irrigate, because water logging and salt build up easily; plough at the right moisture (not too wet, not too dry); make good drainage channels; and add organic matter.

## Laterite soil
Laterite soil forms in hot places with heavy rainfall, where the rain washes away most nutrients and leaves iron and aluminium behind. It is found in the Western Ghats and coastal districts of Karnataka such as Udupi, Dakshina Kannada, Uttara Kannada, Shivamogga, Kodagu and parts of Chikkamagaluru. It is red to brown, porous, acidic and low in nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium, calcium and organic matter. It hardens like brick when exposed and dried. Crops: coffee, tea, rubber, arecanut, coconut, cashew, pepper and spices, with paddy in the valleys. Care: add organic manure, correct strong acidity with lime only as advised after a soil test, protect the soil from erosion with cover crops and terraces on slopes.

## Alluvial soil
Alluvial soil is deposited by rivers and the sea. It covers the large Indo-Gangetic plains of north India and the river deltas and coastal plains, and in Karnataka it is found along rivers and the coast. It is soft, easy to plough and usually fertile, rich in potash but often low in nitrogen and phosphorus. Crops: paddy, wheat, sugarcane, pulses, oilseeds, vegetables, banana and coconut.

## Sandy and arid soil
Sandy and desert soils have big particles, hold very little water and nutrients, and are low in organic matter. They are common in dry regions of Rajasthan and in coastal sand strips. Crops: millets such as bajra, pulses like moth bean, and coconut and cashew on coastal sands. Care: add organic matter, use mulch, drip irrigation and windbreaks.

## Forest and mountain soil
Forest and mountain soils are found on hills with forests, like the Western Ghats. They are rich in organic matter (humus) but are often acidic and thin on slopes. Crops: coffee, tea, spices, cardamom and fruit trees. Care: keep tree cover and terraces to stop erosion.

## Saline and alkaline soil
Saline soils have too much salt, often seen as a white crust on the surface; alkaline (sodic) soils have high pH, are hard when dry and sticky when wet, and water stands on them. They are common in irrigated canal areas with poor drainage, for example parts of the Tungabhadra and Upper Krishna command areas in Karnataka. Most crops grow poorly on them. Care: improve drainage, leach the salts with good quality water, add organic matter, grow salt-tolerant crops, and use gypsum for alkaline soil only as advised after a soil test.

## Soil texture: sandy, loamy and clay
Texture means the size of the soil particles. Sandy soil feels gritty, does not stick together and dries fast. Clay soil feels smooth and sticky when wet, holds water for a long time and cracks when dry. Loamy soil is a mix of sand, silt and clay, holds enough water and air, and is the best for most crops. A simple test: wet a little soil and roll it in the palm; sandy soil falls apart, loam makes a ball that breaks when pressed, and clay makes a long, smooth ribbon.

## Soil pH (acidic, neutral, alkaline)
Soil pH tells if the soil is acidic or alkaline. Most crops grow best in soil that is slightly acidic to neutral, about pH 6.5 to 7.5. Acidic soils (below pH 6.5) are common in high rainfall areas like the Malnad and coastal districts; alkaline soils (above pH 7.5) are common in black soils and in dry, irrigated areas. pH changes how well a crop can take up nutrients, so the correction, lime for acidic soil or gypsum for alkaline soil, should be decided after a soil test.

## How to know your soil type
The best way is a soil test. Collect soil from several places in the field, mix it, and take it to the nearest soil testing laboratory, Raitha Samparka Kendra or Krishi Vigyan Kendra. Under the Soil Health Card scheme the farmer gets a card with the soil's pH, organic carbon and nutrients and the fertilizer advice for the crop. The kiosk's fertilizer screen also uses district soil survey ratings when the soil has not been tested.
